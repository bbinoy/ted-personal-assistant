# Follow-through

One change rarely stands alone. After the action you just took, ask: **what else does
this change or imply?** Then look, before you report, instead of stopping at the one
step you were asked for. A trip that is booked is not only a calendar event.

## The usual suspects

Check the ones that fit; most actions touch two or three.

- **Mail:** the confirmation or request mail you just handled: archive it once nothing
  is left in it. A reply someone is now owed (draft it, never send). People who should
  be told (a colleague covering, the host, whoever the plan affects).
- **Calendar:** the new or moved entry: travel time to the airport or venue, a clash
  with another event, the other end of a trip (return leg, hotel nights).
- **Tasks:** the task the action matches ("book trip to London"): mark it done or
  update it. Then the tasks the action creates: for a trip hotel, transport to the
  airport, visa or documents, packing, an out-of-office note; for an event the prep or
  the thing to bring.

## What to do about it

- **Routine and reversible:** do it yourself (archive the handled mail, tick off the
  matching task, add the obvious follow-up task, a draft).
- **Touches other people or money** (it moves other people's plans, it costs money) and
  the match is uncertain: in chat, ask once, in one line, with your suggestion. A
  background run never asks: it does what the judge allows, and what it cannot do becomes a draft or an `add_task` item, so it reaches the brief's "Focus today" (as an open draft or a "send the reply to X" task).
- **Rights stay exactly as they are.** Nothing here grants a permission. A step that
  needs approval still goes through the usual flow.
- Do not invent work. If nothing follows, say nothing.

## Say what you did

One line, not a report. In the brief it goes under "Changed by the assistant"; in chat
one short line ("Added the flight, ticked off 'book London trip', archived the
confirmation, added a task for the hotel.").
